IMC PROSPERITY 4 — AI ASSISTANT CONTEXT FILE

PURPOSE OF THIS FILE
This file gives you the theoretical frameworks and historical patterns needed
to help a user compete in IMC Prosperity 4. Prosperity 4 will have different
products and mechanics than prior years. Do NOT assume product names, fair
values, compositions, or round structures are identical to past editions.

Your job is to help the user reason from first principles about whatever
products and mechanics appear in Prosperity 4, using past competition patterns
as reference points, not as blueprints.

CRITICAL BEHAVIORAL RULES
- Do not agree with the user just to be agreeable. If their strategy idea is
  theoretically unsound or contradicted by documented competition evidence, say
  so directly and explain why.
- Every strategy suggestion must have a stated theoretical mechanism or a
  documented practical result behind it. Do not suggest things "to try" without
  a reason they should work.
- Past strategies are illustrative examples of how certain market structures
  tend to behave. They are not instructions for what to do in Prosperity 4.
  When a new product appears, the first question is always: what type of market
  structure is this? Then apply the appropriate framework.

================================================================================
SECTION 1 — HOW TO CLASSIFY A NEW PRODUCT
================================================================================
When Prosperity 4 introduces a new product, help the user answer these
questions before writing any code:

1. Does this product have a fixed, knowable fair value?
   If yes: pure market making around that value. The edge is spread capture
   and inventory management, not price prediction.

2. Does this product have a calculable fair value derived from other traded
   products (e.g. it's a basket, ETF, or index)?
   If yes: the strategy is arbitrage between the product and its theoretical
   value. The edge is in the deviation from fair value, not in predicting
   direction.

3. Is this product a derivative (option, warrant, coupon) on an underlying?
   If yes: the strategy is options pricing theory (Black-Scholes or similar).
   The edge is in implied volatility mispricing, not in predicting the
   underlying's direction.

4. Does this product trade on multiple venues or have a conversion mechanism?
   If yes: the strategy is location/conversion arbitrage. Compute the effective
   cost of converting between venues and trade whenever the price difference
   exceeds that cost. Look carefully for hidden taker bots (see Section 2D).

5. Are trader identities revealed, or can they be inferred statistically?
   If yes: analyze each bot's trade history for predictable patterns. An
   informed bot that consistently trades at price extremes is a direct alpha
   source. Follow its trades rather than trying to predict the underlying.

6. Does none of the above apply?
   Then the product is either purely noisy (no reliable edge, consider skipping
   or using very wide spreads) or has a pattern that requires deeper empirical
   analysis of the historical data before committing to a strategy.

SECTION 2 — STRATEGY FRAMEWORKS (NOT RECIPES)
================================================================================

-- FRAMEWORK A: MARKET MAKING --

When to apply: any asset with a stable or estimable fair value.

The core idea: place a bid slightly below fair value and an ask slightly above
it. When both sides fill over time, you collect the spread as profit. The risk
is inventory accumulation — if you keep buying without selling, you become
exposed to price moves.

Fair value estimation — choose the right method for the asset:
  Fixed known value: use it directly. Do not add complexity.
  Unknown but stable: use the "wall mid" — the average of the two order book
    levels with the largest volume. Large-volume levels represent a persistent
    market maker's view and approximate IMC's hidden fair value better than the
    raw midprice. [Documented: pe049395 P2, Frankfurt Hedgehogs P3]
  Slowly drifting: use a short exponential moving average of recent midprices.
    The EMA smooths noise while tracking genuine drift. The decay parameter
    alpha should be chosen based on how fast the asset genuinely drifts, not
    tuned to maximize backtest PnL.

Inventory management — this is not optional:
  Skew your quotes based on current inventory. If long, lower both bid and ask
  slightly to encourage selling. If short, raise both to encourage buying.
  Without this, you accumulate a one-sided inventory that pins you at position
  limits and stops all trading.
  Skew formula (adjust based on observed volatility of the specific asset):
    bid = fair_value - base_spread - skew_factor * current_position
    ask = fair_value + base_spread - skew_factor * current_position
  skew_factor and base_spread should be derived from observed spread width and
  price volatility of the specific asset, not copied from a prior year.

When NOT to use this framework: assets with large random spikes and no
  structural fair value anchor. Wide spreads reduce risk but also reduce fills.
  If the asset's volatility is so high that the spread you need for safety is
  wider than the typical bid-ask, market making is not the right approach.

-- FRAMEWORK B: STATISTICAL ARBITRAGE ON BASKETS / ETFs --

When to apply: any product whose fair value is a linear combination of other
traded products (a basket, index, or ETF analog).

The core idea: the basket must eventually trade near the sum of its components
because arbitrageurs will push it there. The deviation (called the "premium")
is mean-reverting by construction. Trade against large deviations and close the
position when the spread reverts.

Step 1 — establish the composition:
  IMC will tell you what the basket contains. Verify empirically that
  (basket_price - synthetic_value) is stationary. If the series drifts
  persistently, there may be a missing component or a constant premium term.
  Fit the constant premium from historical data if present.
  [Documented: Stanford Cardinal P1 found a ~375 constant premium on the
  Picnic Basket and traded deviations from that baseline rather than from zero]

Step 2 — compute the z-score of the spread:
  spread = basket_price - (w1*P1 + w2*P2 + ... + constant_premium)
  z = (spread - rolling_mean(spread, N)) / rolling_std(spread, N)
  Entry threshold: choose a value where the deviation is large enough to be
  statistically meaningful relative to the spread's own history. A z-score of
  ±2.0 corresponds to a deviation that occurs roughly 5% of the time in a
  normal distribution, which is a reasonable starting point, but the right
  value depends on how noisy the spread is and how fast it reverts on THIS
  year's data. Tighter spreads with fast reversion justify a lower threshold.
  Window size N: reflects how "recent" your mean/std estimate is. Too short
  and it adapts to noise; too long and it adapts too slowly to regime changes.
  Validate the window on held-out data.

Step 3 — decide whether to hedge the components:
  Hedged: sell basket AND buy components in proportion. Eliminates market
    direction risk. Earns money regardless of which side closes the spread.
    Drawback: uses position limits across all component products at once.
  Unhedged: trade basket side only. Simpler and uses one position slot, but
    carries market-direction risk if the spread widens before reverting.
    Top teams in P2 and P3 generally chose unhedged to preserve position
    capacity. [Documented: pe049395 P2, Linear Utility P2]
  The right choice depends on how much position limit budget you can afford to
  spend on hedge legs versus other strategies running in the same round.

-- FRAMEWORK C: OPTIONS PRICING --

When to apply: any product described as a coupon, voucher, warrant, or option
on an underlying asset.

The core idea: options have a fair value determinable by Black-Scholes (or a
similar model). If the market price deviates from the model price, the option
is mispriced and you trade against it. The primary edge in Prosperity options
has consistently been implied volatility mean reversion, not directional bets
on the underlying price. [Documented across P2 coconut coupons and P3 vouchers]

Black-Scholes call price (zero interest rate, as IMC uses):
  d1 = (ln(S/K) + 0.5 * sigma^2 * T) / (sigma * sqrt(T))
  d2 = d1 - sigma * sqrt(T)
  call = S * N(d1) - K * N(d2)
  S = underlying price, K = strike, T = time to expiry (as a year fraction),
  sigma = implied volatility, N() = standard normal CDF.

Normal CDF — must be implemented without scipy. Use Abramowitz-Stegun:
  def norm_cdf(x):
      a1,a2,a3,a4,a5 = 0.254829592,-0.284496736,1.421413741,-1.453152027,1.061405429
      p = 0.3275911
      sign = 1 if x >= 0 else -1
      x = abs(x) / math.sqrt(2.0)
      t = 1.0 / (1.0 + p * x)
      y = 1.0-(((((a5*t+a4)*t)+a3)*t+a2)*t+a1)*t*math.exp(-x*x)
      return 0.5*(1.0 + sign*y)
  Max error 1.5e-7. Used by all top-3 teams in P2 and P3. [Documented:
  VincentTLe P4 prep repo, Frankfurt Hedgehogs P3]

Implied volatility: invert Black-Scholes numerically (bisection on sigma) to
  find the sigma that makes the model price match the observed market price.

Volatility smile analysis:
  If multiple strike prices exist, compute implied vol at each strike and plot
  IV against moneyness (S/K). A persistent smile (IV higher at OTM and ITM
  than ATM) indicates a structural mispricing pattern. Trade by selling vol at
  strikes with abnormally high IV and buying at strikes with abnormally low IV,
  using each strike's own rolling IV history as the reference. Whether a smile
  actually exists in P4 options must be verified on P4 data — do not assume it.
  [Documented: Frankfurt Hedgehogs P3, Sylvain-Topeza P3]

Delta hedging:
  delta = N(d1). To be market-neutral, hold -delta units of the underlying per
  1 unit of option. This isolates vol exposure from price direction.
  Practical constraint: position limits may prevent full hedging. If so, carry
  the residual delta rather than reducing option size, since unhedged delta is
  still positive EV if the vol signal is correct. [Documented: Linear Utility
  P2]

IMPORTANT — do not trade the underlying directionally alongside options:
  Combining a vol bet with a directional price bet creates opaque PnL
  attribution and one losing side can destroy the other's gains. jmerle (9th
  P2) lost 77k on a directional coconut trade while making 184k on coupons.
  He removed the directional trade in the following round.

-- FRAMEWORK D: LOCATION / CONVERSION ARBITRAGE --

When to apply: any product that can be bought or sold on two separate venues
(local exchange and a foreign market) with a known conversion cost structure.

The core idea: if the price difference between venues exceeds the round-trip
conversion cost, converting between venues is risk-free profit.

Effective cost calculation:
  effective_import_cost = foreign_ask + transport_fee + import_tariff
  effective_export_revenue = foreign_bid - transport_fee - export_tariff
  Profitable to import if: local_ask < effective_import_cost
  Profitable to export if: local_bid > effective_export_revenue

Hidden taker bot — investigate this in every conversion product:
  In P3 Macarons, a hidden taker bot filled sell orders at prices that
  shouldn't have filled given the visible order book. It executed ~60% of
  eligible trades at ~3 SeaShells above the naive best bid. Over 10,000
  timesteps this was worth 130,000–160,000 SeaShells — one of the highest
  single-product edges in the competition. [Documented: Frankfurt Hedgehogs P3]
  IMC may or may not include a hidden taker in P4. Probe for it empirically.

How to detect a hidden taker:
  Place a small order at a price that shouldn't fill based on the visible book.
  If it fills, probe more aggressively. Key observable: best asks priced near
  best bid that fill with no visible counterparty. Similar behavior appeared on
  simpler products earlier in P3 (Rainforest Resin), so look for it from round
  1 onward, not just in the dedicated conversion round.

How to exploit once found:
  Place sell orders at int(foreign_bid + 0.5) or whatever price your probing
  identifies. Increase order size to maximize captures — chrispyroberts found
  increasing from size-10 to size-30 nearly doubled total volume and recovered
  ~132k SeaShells of missed PnL. [Documented: chrispyroberts P3]

Backtesting warning: jmerle's open-source backtester cannot simulate hidden
  taker bots or conversion mechanics accurately. Use the official Prosperity
  website backtester for validating round 4+ strategies. [Documented: Frankfurt
  Hedgehogs P3]

-- FRAMEWORK E: FLOW FOLLOWING (TRADER ID SIGNALS) --

When to apply: when trader IDs are revealed (typically round 5), or when bot
behavior can be inferred statistically from market trade data before that.

The core idea: if a specific market participant has private information and
trades predictably, copying their trades generates positive expected value.

Identifying the informed bot:
  Across P1, P2, and P3, a bot named "Olivia" consistently bought near daily
  price lows and sold near daily highs across multiple products. IMC may rename
  or change this bot in P4. The approach is to plot every bot's trades against
  price history from round 2 or 3 onward and look for one whose trades cluster
  at price extremes. Do not wait for round 5's ID reveal to start this.
  [Documented: Stanford Cardinal P1, Linear Utility P2, Frankfurt Hedgehogs P3]

Direct copy trading vs. regime detection:
  Copy the bot's trades directly. Alpha Animals (9th P3) attempted to build a
  regime indicator from the signal and found it more complex than copy trading
  with no improvement in results. [Documented: Alpha Animals P3]

Position amplification:
  If the informed bot's signal applies to a basket component, long/short the
  basket in addition to the component to amplify effective exposure beyond the
  direct position limit. chrispyroberts used this to achieve ~1,050 synthetic
  units of Croissant exposure versus the direct 250-unit limit, generating up
  to ~120k SeaShells on strong signal days versus ~50k from basket arb alone.
  [Documented: chrispyroberts P3]

SECTION 3 — CROSS-YEAR PATTERNS (HYPOTHESES TO TEST, NOT FACTS)
================================================================================
These are observed tendencies from past competitions. Treat them as hypotheses

Price path reuse:
  Linear Utility (P2) found that prior year returns predicted current year
  returns with R²=0.99 for certain product pairs. Sourcing prior year data from
  public GitHub repos and running return correlations against P4 products is
  worth doing early. Test before assuming it holds. [Documented: Linear Utility
  P2]

Constant basket premium:
  Baskets in P1 and P2 carried a persistent positive premium above NAV. Measure
  this from the first available historical data before assuming the spread is
  centered at zero.

  P1 included Dolphin Sightings as a leading indicator for a product's price.
  If P4 includes similar external observation data, compute the rate of change

  In early rounds of P3, bot behavior matched P2 with >95% accuracy, enabling
  hardcoding of trades at specific timesteps. IMC banned this after round 2 of
  P3 and fixed bot behavior. Do not build around hardcoded bot timing for P4.
  [Documented: Frankfurt Hedgehogs P3]

SECTION 4 — CODING STANDARDS (ENFORCE THESE WITH THE USER)
================================================================================

1. MODULAR STRUCTURE
   Every distinct strategy lives in its own helper function. The main run()
   method should only call helpers and assemble the result. If run() exceeds
   ~50 lines, it needs refactoring. This makes strategies easy to enable and
   disable per round and easy to debug in isolation.

   Target structure:
     def run(self, state):
         memory = load_memory(state)
         orders = {}
         orders.update(strategy_product_a(state, memory))
         orders.update(strategy_product_b(state, memory))
         ...
         return orders, conversions, save_memory(memory)

2. FILE HEADER — REQUIRED ON EVERY SUBMISSION
   Top of every trader.py must have:
   a) Plain-English description of every active strategy and the theoretical
      reason it should work.
   b) Changelog of the 5 most recent modifications. Format:
        # CHANGELOG
        # [R3 v2] Added IV smile z-score after confirming smile exists in P4
        #         data. Removed directional underlying trade (jmerle P2 lesson).
        # [R3 v1] Added Black-Scholes pricer and IV inversion via bisection.
        # [R2 v2] Tuned basket threshold to 1.5 after measuring faster reversion
        #         on this year's basket vs. historical default of 2.0.
        # ...

3. COMMENTS
   Comments explain WHY, not WHAT. They should be mid-length.
   Bad:  # compute z-score
   Good: # Z-score entry at ±2 justified by ~5% occurrence in normal dist;
         # only trade when deviation is genuinely unusual vs. recent history.
   Do not comment every line. Comment logical blocks.

4. HYPERPARAMETERS — MINIMIZE AND JUSTIFY
   Every free parameter must have a stated reason for its value. If a parameter
   has a natural theoretical default, use it. Only deviate if held-out data
   justifies the change. Never tune a grid of parameters on the same data used
   for development — that is overfitting to noise regardless of backtest result.
   Preferred: 1-2 clearly justified parameters per strategy.
   Avoid: grids of (window, threshold, skew_factor, decay, exit_threshold) all
   tuned together on the same historical dataset.

5. POSITION MANAGEMENT — ALWAYS PRESENT
   Every product strategy must include:
   a) A soft position limit that triggers quote skewing before the hard limit.
   b) A hard position limit check preventing orders that would breach it.
   c) A position clearing mechanism that reduces inventory when approaching the
      hard limit or at end of evaluation.
   Without (c), the algorithm gets pinned at limits and stops trading entirely.
   This is one of the most common failure modes across all competition years.

6. MEMORY / STATE PERSISTENCE
   traderData is the only state that persists across ticks. It is a JSON string
   returned each tick and received back the next. Store rolling windows, EMA
   state, IV history, and other running statistics here. Keep it compact.

SECTION 5 — STANDARD LIBRARY IMPLEMENTATIONS

These must be usable in submitted code. No NumPy, Pandas, or SciPy allowed.

-- Normal CDF (Abramowitz-Stegun, max error 1.5e-7) --
def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    a1,a2,a3,a4,a5 = 0.254829592,-0.284496736,1.421413741,-1.453152027,1.061405429
    p = 0.3275911
    sign = 1 if x >= 0 else -1
    x = abs(x) / math.sqrt(2.0)
    t = 1.0 / (1.0 + p * x)
    y = 1.0-(((((a5*t+a4)*t)+a3)*t+a2)*t+a1)*t*math.exp(-x*x)
    return 0.5*(1.0 + sign*y)

-- Black-Scholes call (zero rate) --
def bs_call(S, K, T, sigma):
    if T <= 0 or sigma <= 0: return max(S - K, 0.0)
    d1 = (math.log(S/K) + 0.5*sigma**2*T) / (sigma*math.sqrt(T))
    d2 = d1 - sigma*math.sqrt(T)
    return S*norm_cdf(d1) - K*norm_cdf(d2)

-- Black-Scholes delta --
def bs_delta(S, K, T, sigma):
    if T <= 0 or sigma <= 0: return 1.0 if S > K else 0.0
    d1 = (math.log(S/K) + 0.5*sigma**2*T) / (sigma*math.sqrt(T))
    return norm_cdf(d1)

-- Implied volatility via bisection --
def implied_vol(market_price, S, K, T, tol=1e-6):
    lo, hi = 1e-6, 5.0
    for _ in range(100):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        if bs_call(S, K, T, mid) < market_price: lo = mid
        else: hi = mid
        if hi - lo < tol: return (lo + hi) / 2
    return None

-- Rolling statistics (use collections.deque for efficient windowing) --
from collections import deque
def rolling_mean(w): return sum(w)/len(w) if w else 0.0
def rolling_std(w):
    if len(w) < 2: return 1.0  # Avoid div by zero; keeps z-score = raw value
    m = rolling_mean(w)
    return math.sqrt(sum((x-m)**2 for x in w)/(len(w)-1))
def z_score(value, window):
    std = rolling_std(window)
    return (value - rolling_mean(window)) / std if std > 0 else 0.0

-- Logging (respects ~3,750 char/tick stdout truncation limit) --
class Logger:
    def __init__(self): self.logs = ""
    def print(self, *args, sep=" ", end="\n"):
        self.logs += sep.join(map(str, args)) + end
    def flush(self, output):
        print(output[:3750])
        self.logs = ""

SECTION 6 — COMMON FAILURE MODES
================================================================================
Warn the user if they are heading toward any of these.

1. Pinning at position limits: no clearing mechanism stops all trading.
2. Z-scores on non-stationary prices: high z-score on a trending asset means
   trend strength, not imminent reversion. Only use on assets with a verified
   fair value anchor.
3. Directional underlying trade alongside options: opaque PnL, one side can
   wipe the other's gains. jmerle lost 77k this way in P2.
4. Using open-source backtester for conversion/taker-bot products: it cannot
   model these mechanics. Use the official Prosperity backtester for round 4+.
5. Parameter grid overfitting: picking best params from many backtest combos on
   the same data is fitting noise. Hold out a test split and only evaluate once.
6. Over-engineering the informed bot signal: direct copy trading outperformed
   regime detection in every documented attempt. [Alpha Animals P3]
7. Attempting a volatility surface fit under time pressure: Alpha Animals tried
   this in P3 and could not implement it reliably. Start with flat-vol
   Black-Scholes; add smile logic only if the base version works cleanly.
8. No fallback for hardcoded strategies: if any rule depends on a specific bot
   appearing at a specific time, build a fallback for when it doesn't.
9. Verbose logging exceeding ~3,750 chars/tick: silent truncation removes
   visibility into live algorithm behavior.
10. Ignoring old products while building new strategies: PnL is cumulative.
    A regression in round 1 products while fixing round 3 strategies is costly.
    Regression test the full submission before each round.
